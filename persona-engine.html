Solutions · Persona Engine

Personas that are sized, scored, and provable

Not a workshop exercise, and not five generic archetypes. Every persona comes out of a clustering process run against your real customers -- and there are usually a lot more of them than you'd expect.

The problem with a handful of personas

Your persona deck lists five generic customer types. Your CRM has enough real behavioral variety to support fifty distinct, addressable segments -- and nobody's built them, because the tools available only ever hand you a handful.

Most "persona builders" produce three to five broad-strokes profiles because that's what a workshop has time to name and a slide deck has room to hold. That ceiling isn't a data limitation -- it's a tooling limitation. A mid-sized customer file easily contains dozens of statistically distinct behavioral groups, each addressable with different messaging, sitting unused.

How it actually works

Fan out. Cluster. Converge.

Every customer record fans out as its own data point. Similar records pull together by real behavioral similarity, not a guess. Each resulting group converges into one addressable persona -- and there are always several, never just one.

1. Fan out

Every order, click, and email open becomes its own record -- nothing is pre-grouped or assumed.

2. Cluster

Customers who behave alike pull together, and the groupings are validated for real behavioral separation.

3. Converge

Each group large enough to act on becomes one named, addressable persona.

The clustering, in plain terms

Grouping your customers is the easy part — knowing which grouping is actually meaningful is not. The engine validates that the segments it surfaces separate on real buying behavior rather than surface-level similarity.

Groups too small to build a campaign around never become a persona. There's no strategic value in a segment you can't act on, however distinct it looks on a chart.

AI writes it. Data proves it.

The AI authors the name, the story, the pain points, and the voice notes. Every number underneath -- segment size, purchase intent, conversion propensity, channel affinity, priority -- comes directly from your data, not the model's imagination.

Personas are re-cut as new data arrives, so the list grows and shifts as your customer base does, instead of freezing the day it's written.

Why this model exists

Twenty-seven years of building personas — and watching where they break

There's nothing secret about clustering. The techniques are well documented and any competent data scientist can run them. What's hard is knowing which signals actually predict buying behavior, where the standard approaches quietly fail, and what a persona needs to contain before a marketer can write from it. That only comes from doing it, at scale, for a long time.

27

Years building personas for real brands

Not in a lab or a research vendor's methodology deck — inside working marketing programs for national and global brands, where a persona that didn't move revenue got abandoned within a quarter. Enough cycles to learn which ones held up and which ones were just well-presented guesses.

Built alongside client data teams, not around them

Direct access to the actual data — warehouses, CRM exports, order tables, analytics platforms — working with the analysts who owned it. That access is what surfaced the failure points you can't see from the outside: identity fragmentation, segments too small to act on, clusters that looked clean and predicted nothing.

Three disciplines that rarely sit together

Data science knows how to cluster but not what marketers need. Analytics knows what happened but not what to write. Marketing knows the message but not the math. Most persona work fails in the gaps between those three. This model was built by someone who had to operate in all three at once, for decades.

Finding the gaps, then filling them

Every persona engagement over those years surfaced the same recurring failures. Segments that were statistically distinct but too small to build a campaign around. Personas that looked convincing in a deck and had no traceable connection to the customer file. Groupings that separated cleanly on demographics and not at all on purchase behavior. The same person counted three times because nobody resolved identity first. Decks that were accurate the week they shipped and obsolete two quarters later.

None of those are exotic problems. They're the ordinary ways persona work goes wrong, and they repeat across companies and categories because the process is manual and nobody systematizes the lessons. After enough repetitions, the fixes stopped being one-off patches and became a method — a set of standards about what a persona has to clear before anyone writes a word against it.

That method is what Triangul8 automates. Not a secret algorithm — a set of hard-won decisions about how to do this correctly, encoded so it runs consistently on your data instead of depending on who happens to be in the room.

The ten-person focus group

Ten people in a room, paid to have opinions, told they're being observed. The findings get treated as representative of a customer base of two hundred thousand. Focus groups are genuinely useful for understanding language and emotional reaction, and they are statistically meaningless for segmentation. Ten opinions cannot describe a market.

With
